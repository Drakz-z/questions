8.3 Answers with explanations
P1 c      P2 a      P3 d      P4 b      P5 b      P6 d      P7 a      P8 c      P9 d      P10 b      P11 a      P12 c      P13 c      P14 a      P15 b      P16 d
P1 – c.  A bakery whose orders have doubled needs more staff than one baker can provide
The stated reason (orders doubled) only supports hiring if more orders mean one baker can’t cope. That unstated link is the implicit premise. The other options add ideas the argument never uses.
P2 – a.  “So your point is that uniforms limit students’ chance to express who they are, which matters for their development. Let me explain where I think that goes wrong.”
Charity = restate the argument in its strongest reasonable form, then engage with it. The second option is a straw man, the third attacks the person instead of the argument, and the fourth is prejudice (deciding in advance).
P3 – d.  “Anyone under 18 needs a signed permission form. Mia is under 18.”
The conclusion (“Mia needs a signed form”) only follows when both premises are taken together – drop either and nothing follows. In the other pairs each premise gives its own reason, so they are independent.
P4 – b.  “Their logo is awful”
A logo has no bearing on whether a supplier is reliable, so it is left out of the standard form. The conclusion is “switch suppliers” and the lateness is the premise.
P5 – b.  Is an assumption being made about one thing being the cause of another when this is not obviously true
The argument assumes that because B followed A, A caused B. Challenge question 2 targets exactly that. Other things (season, advertising) may explain the rise.
P6 – d.  The website could be unavailable for other reasons, so affirming the consequent doesn’t prove the antecedent
This is affirming the consequent. The rule says server down → website unavailable, not the reverse, so other causes (maintenance, an expired domain) are possible.
P7 – a.  Denying the consequent, a valid form
Given “if P then Q”, if Q is false then P must be false. That is denying the consequent (modus tollens) and it is valid.
P8 – c.  Necessary but not sufficient
Every dog is a mammal, so a dog can’t exist without being a mammal (necessary). But being a mammal isn’t enough – cats and whales are mammals too (not sufficient).
P9 – d.  A valid argument can have a false conclusion if at least one of its premises is false
Validity concerns structure only. With a false premise a valid argument may yield a false conclusion – it is then unsound. A sound argument (valid + true premises) must have a true conclusion.
P10 – b.  The sample is drawn only from people who use the library late at night, so it doesn’t represent all students
This is a form of specific areapre-screening bias only late-night library users are included, while students who never use the library or use it by day are excluded.
P11 – a.  It rests on a sample of one (n = 1), which is an anecdote, not a serious investigation
One case can’t stand for a whole category. Any inductive argument based on a single instance is very weak.
P12 – c.  The employee is a manager
Each added detail makes the scenario more specific, and a specific scenario can never be more likely than the general one it belongs to.
P13 – c.  Cogent but not inductively forceful
Cogent = good structure. Inductively forceful = good structure AND true premises. With a false premise we have no good reason to accept the conclusion. (“Sound” and “invalid” are deductive terms.)
P14 – a.  A black swan event that falsified the generalisation “prices never fall nationwide”
The event defied previous experience and the expectations based on it. Forty years of confirming cases never proved the generalisation – one counter-event disproved it (the problem of induction).
P15 – b.  Cats usually (but not invariably) land on their feet
A general statement is rarely meant literally; the implicit qualification indicates the frequency with which it applies – here “usually” or “nearly always”.
P16 – d.  An equal chance of winning or losing, since previous tosses don’t affect it
Rational expectation is what it is most reasonable to expect, which can differ from what someone personally expects. For a fair coin each toss is independent, so the chance remains 5050. (Extra explanation this is the “gambler’s fallacy”.)
